# Grant writing

Write like a busy human expert: answer directly, supply enough context, then stop. Save the reader's effort as well as the writer's. Concise means no wasted words, not missing information.

## Workflow

1. Read the actual application form and funding brief, including examples, eligibility, help text, limits, and options. Match questions one-to-one in their original order. If the form cannot be read, request screenshots or pasted questions rather than inventing fields.
2. Check that the proposed work genuinely fits the funding criteria. Explain a mismatch; repeating funder terminology does not resolve it.
3. Separate confirmed facts, estimates, proposed activities, and unknowns. Use primary sources for external claims. Preserve study limitations. Ask targeted questions about the applicant's experience; a community website does not establish personal credentials. Keep unknown required fields visibly unresolved outside paste-ready answers.
4. Draft each answer using the contract below. Use skill `humanizer` for prose refinement. Plain-text answer blocks are editable prose, not executable code; preserve their wrappers and factual values.
5. Verify field coverage, facts, budgets, dates, agenda timing, and reading order. Re-read current files before editing. Apply only the requested changes.

## Answer contract

| Question | Answer contains |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who, how many, recruitment channels, applicant's connection |
| Outcomes | What people understand or do differently, tangible output, feasible follow-up |
| Plan | Format, timing, discussion topics, roles, confirmation status |
| Qualifications | Relevant experience and concrete results |
| Costs | Quantities, rates, arithmetic, free contributions, total |
| Without funding | Ongoing work explained briefly; what changes for this project |

### Read in question order

Treat the document as a sequential conversation. At question N, the reader knows only the introduction and questions 1 through N-1.

An answer may introduce information needed to answer its own question. It must not assume information explained only later. Give a new concept enough context on first mention; leave operational detail in its proper question.

Example: outcomes precede the agenda. Write “Participants will propose a safeguard for a workflow they use.” Introduce the small-group format in the agenda before saying “each group.”

Scan for unintroduced references: “the groups,” “this approach,” “these activities,” “our ongoing work.” Name the referent or remove the dependency. If context appeared much earlier, a short identifying phrase is enough.

### Efficient, complete answers

Start with the answer, then necessary evidence or explanation. Usually a few sentences suffice; agendas and budgets need lists. Let concrete experience demonstrate credibility. Remove repeated motivation, defensive caveats, and ceremonial conclusions.

“Our work continues” is too vague. “We will continue sharing Vietnamese AI safety resources and hosting Discord discussions; this event needs another funding source” gives the reader enough information.

Do not invent metrics, partnerships, fallback plans, or commitments to sound persuasive. Label suggested targets as proposals.

## Budget and file checks

- Count participants, organizers, and speakers when pricing catering. Distinguish preparation hours from total hours, and per-person hours from combined hours. A proposed hourly rate is not a funding standard.
- Show exact costs separately from required rounding; explain any allowance.
- Follow the user's chosen source of truth. Word-to-Markdown synchronization preserves intentional edits and blanks. Flag inconsistencies without silently resolving them.
- Export only when requested; verify the output. Never overwrite newer Word edits during a prose-only Markdown revision.
